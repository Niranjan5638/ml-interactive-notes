Chapter 14

Hierarchical Clustering

Building a nested tree of clusters bottom-up or top-down, and reading it as a dendrogram.

Textbook-supplied

Your class notes list hierarchical clustering as agglomerative and divisive but never develop it. This chapter is built from Alpaydin chapter 7.

A tree instead of a partition

k-means, DBSCAN and OPTICS all produce a flat partition: each point gets one label. Hierarchical clustering produces something richer — a nested sequence of clusterings, from every point alone at the bottom to everything together at the top. You then cut the tree at whatever level gives the number of clusters you want.

DirectionStarts withRepeats until
Agglomerative (bottom-up)n singleton clustersMerge the two closest clusters → one cluster remains
Divisive (top-down)One cluster containing everythingSplit a cluster into two → n singletons remain

Agglomerative is by far the more common, because splitting a cluster optimally is harder than merging two. The rest of this chapter is about agglomerative.

Linkage — how to measure distance between clusters

The algorithm needs a distance between clusters, not just points. Four choices, and the choice changes the result:

LinkageDistance between clusters A and BCharacter
Single (min)\(\min\{d(a,b) : a\in A, b\in B\}\)Can follow chains and find elongated shapes; prone to chaining noise into one cluster
Complete (max)\(\max\{d(a,b)\}\)Produces compact, roughly equal clusters; sensitive to outliers
Average (UPGMA)mean of all cross-pair distancesCompromise between the two
Wardmerge that increases within-cluster variance leastUsually the best default; produces balanced, spherical clusters

Solved example 1 — single linkage

Points A = 2, B = 5, C = 9, D = 15 on a line

Pairwise distances: \(d(A,B)=3\), \(d(A,C)=7\), \(d(A,D)=13\), \(d(B,C)=4\), \(d(B,D)=10\), \(d(C,D)=6\).

Step 1. Closest pair is A–B at distance 3. Merge into {A,B}, record height 3.

Step 2. With single linkage, the distance from {A,B} to anything is the minimum over its members:

  • \(d(\{A,B\}, C) = \min(7, 4) = 4\)
  • \(d(\{A,B\}, D) = \min(13, 10) = 10\)
  • \(d(C, D) = 6\)

Closest is {A,B}–C at 4. Merge, height 4.

Step 3. \(d(\{A,B,C\}, D) = \min(10, 6) = 6\). Merge, height 6.

Result: merges at heights 3, 4, 6. Cutting at height 5 gives two clusters {A,B,C} and {D}; cutting at height 4 gives {A,B} and {C,D}… no — at height 4 the merge of C has just happened, so cutting just below 4 gives {A,B}, {C}, {D}: three clusters.

Solved example 2 — the same points with complete linkage

Showing that linkage changes the answer

Step 1. Closest pair is still A–B at 3. Merge, height 3.

Step 2. With complete linkage the distance is the maximum over members:

  • \(d(\{A,B\}, C) = \max(7, 4) = 7\)
  • \(d(\{A,B\}, D) = \max(13, 10) = 13\)
  • \(d(C, D) = 6\)

Now the closest pair is C–D at 6, not {A,B}–C. Merge C and D, height 6.

Step 3. \(d(\{A,B\}, \{C,D\}) = \max(7, 13, 4, 10) = 13\). Merge, height 13.

Result: merges at heights 3, 6, 13.

Why the two disagree, and which to trust

Single linkage merged C into {A,B} early because C was close to one member (B, at 4). Complete linkage refused, because C is far from every member of the other cluster (7 from A). This is the chaining problem in miniature: single linkage will happily build long chains through intermediate points, which is good for elongated shapes and bad for noisy data.

The practical rule: Ward for compact clusters, single when you expect irregular shapes and trust your data to be clean, complete when you fear outliers.

Reading a dendrogram

A dendrogram is the tree drawn with the merge height on the vertical axis. Three things to read from it:

  • Where to cut. A horizontal line at height \(h\) gives the clustering at that similarity level. The number of vertical lines it crosses is the number of clusters.
  • Long vertical branches indicate a large jump in dissimilarity — a natural place to cut, because it means two groups were genuinely far apart before merging.
  • The order along the bottom is arbitrary with respect to the original data; adjacent leaves are similar, but the left-to-right order carries no meaning beyond that.

The cost that limits it

Agglomerative clustering needs the full pairwise distance matrix — \(O(n^2)\) memory — and \(n-1\) merges, each requiring a search for the closest pair. On 100,000 points that matrix alone is ten billion entries. Hierarchical clustering is for hundreds or a few thousand points, not millions. That is its binding limitation.

Check your understanding

In solved example 1, why did C join {A,B} before D did?
Single linkage defines cluster distance as the minimum over all cross pairs. C's distance to B is 4, which is less than D's 6 to C and 10 to B. The individual values of the points are irrelevant — only the distances matter. With complete linkage the same C would have been rejected because it is 7 from A.
