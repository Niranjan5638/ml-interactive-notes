Chapter 13

OPTICS

Ordering points by reachability so clusters of different densities all show up.

Self-study topic

Your notes mark OPTICS as "study on our own". This chapter is built from the original Ankerst et al. formulation and the treatment in Han and Kamber.

The problem with a global eps

Chapter 12 ended on the limitation: DBSCAN uses one ε for the whole dataset, so it cannot handle a dataset containing both a tight cluster and a loose one. OPTICS keeps DBSCAN's density definitions but removes the requirement to commit to ε. Instead of producing clusters directly, it produces an ordering of the points together with a reachability distance for each — and clusters are then read off the resulting plot, at whatever density you choose.

The two new quantities

Definitions

Core distance of a point \(p\) — the distance to its MinPts-th nearest neighbour (counting \(p\) itself). If \(p\) has fewer than MinPts neighbours within ε, its core distance is undefined and \(p\) is not a core point.

Reachability distance from \(o\) to \(p\):

\[ \text{rd}(p, o) = \begin{cases} \text{undefined} & \text{if } o \text{ is not core} \\ \max\bigl(\text{core-dist}(o),\ d(o,p)\bigr) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

The max is the clever part. If \(p\) is very close to a core point \(o\), the reachability distance is still at least \(o\)'s core distance — so a dense region produces uniformly low reachability values, and a sparse region produces high ones, regardless of the individual pairwise distances.

The algorithm, step by step

  1. Set ε (an upper bound, usually generous) and MinPts.
  2. For every point, compute its core distance.
  3. Maintain a priority queue of unprocessed points keyed by reachability distance.
  4. Pick any unprocessed point as the start of a new ordering. Write it to the output with reachability undefined.
  5. If it is core, update the reachability of each of its ε-neighbours: rd = max(core-dist(current), d(current, neighbour)), keeping the minimum over all updates seen so far.
  6. Pop the unprocessed point with the smallest reachability, write it to the output with that value, and repeat step 5 from it.
  7. When the queue empties, start again from any remaining unprocessed point (its reachability is undefined — it begins a new cluster).

The output is a list of (point, reachability) pairs. Plotted in order, this is the reachability plot: valleys are clusters, peaks are separations, and tall spikes are noise.

Reachability values produced (using the max rule):

  • Within the dense cluster: values between 0.5 and 1.5. For example from 1 (core distance 1.0) to 1.5 the reachability is max(1.0, 0.5) = 1.0; from 1.5 (core distance 0.5) to 2 it is max(0.5, 0.5) = 0.5.
  • Within the sparse cluster: from 10 (core distance 4.0) to 12 the reachability is max(4.0, 2.0) = 4.0; from 12 (core distance 2.0) to 14 it is max(2.0, 2.0) = 2.0.

The reachability plot therefore shows two valleys of very different depth: roughly 0.5–1.5 for the first cluster and 2.0–4.0 for the second, separated by a large gap where the ordering jumps between them.

Why this matters. Run DBSCAN on the same data with ε = 2 and MinPts = 3. The dense cluster is found — point 1 has four neighbours within 2. But point 10 has only {10, 12} within 2, which is fewer than 3, so the entire sparse cluster is labelled noise. OPTICS, with the same MinPts and a generous ε of 5, reports both valleys and lets you decide the cut afterwards.

The key sentence for an exam answer

DBSCAN answers "what are the clusters at density ε?" — you must commit to ε before you see the answer. OPTICS answers "how does density vary across the data?" — the ordering contains results for all ε simultaneously, so you can extract clusters at whatever density threshold suits each region.

Extracting clusters from the ordering

Once you have the reachability plot, clusters are the valleys. Practical extraction methods:

  • Fixed threshold — cut the plot at a height \(\varepsilon'\); contiguous runs below it are clusters. This reproduces DBSCAN at that ε, but you can choose \(\varepsilon'\) after seeing the data.
  • Steepness / ξ — the original paper's method: find steep downward and upward slopes in the plot and use them to place cluster boundaries. This detects valleys of any depth, which is what makes it density-independent.
  • Hierarchical extraction — treat nested valleys as nested clusters, giving a dendrogram-like hierarchy.

Check your understanding

In solved example 1, why is the reachability of point 2 equal to 2 and not 1?
The distance d(1,2) is only 1, but point 1's core distance is 2, and reachability is the max of the two. That floor is deliberate: it makes every point in a sparse neighbourhood report a high value even when an individual pair happens to be close.
What does a tall spike in a reachability plot indicate?
Reachability is low inside a dense region and jumps when the ordering moves to a point far from anything previously seen. Valleys are clusters; the rises between them are separations, and an isolated point produces the tallest spike of all.
