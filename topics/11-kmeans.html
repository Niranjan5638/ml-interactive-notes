Chapter 11

k-Means Clustering

Partitioning points into k groups by alternating assignment and centroid updates.

The problem k-means solves

You have data with no labels and you believe it falls into groups. k-means answers: given k, what is the best way to partition these points into k groups?

Formal statement

Given \(n\) points \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and an integer \(k\), find \(k\) centroids \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_k\) and an assignment of each point to one centroid, minimising the within-cluster sum of squares:

\[ \text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\ \sum_{x \in C_i} \|x - \mu_i\|^2 \]

where \(C_i\) is the set of points assigned to centroid \(i\), and \(\mu_i = \frac{1}{|C_i|}\sum_{x\in C_i} x\) is their mean.

SSE is also called inertia in scikit-learn. It is the sum of squared distances from each point to its own centroid, so smaller is tighter.

Why this objective, and why it is hard

Finding the globally optimal partition is NP-hard — the number of ways to split \(n\) points into \(k\) groups is a Stirling number, astronomically large. k-means does not solve that problem. It finds a local optimum by alternating two easy steps, which is fast and usually good enough.

The algorithm, step by step

  1. Choose k and initialise k centroids. Randomly, or by k-means++ (pick each new centroid with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest existing centroid — this is what scikit-learn does by default and it reduces bad local optima).
  2. Assignment step. For every point, compute the distance to every centroid and assign the point to the nearest one.
  3. Update step. Recompute each centroid as the mean of the points now assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the assignments stop changing (or a maximum iteration count is reached).
  5. Report the final centroids and assignments, and the SSE.
Why it must terminate

Each step can only decrease or hold the SSE: assigning each point to its nearest centroid is the best assignment given the centroids, and replacing a centroid by the mean of its members is the best centroid given the assignment. Since there are finitely many possible assignments, the procedure cannot cycle forever — it reaches a fixed point in a finite number of iterations. It may reach a local optimum, not the global one.

What that trace shows

Notice how long convergence took — five iterations for nine points — and that points 4, 10 and 12 each changed cluster after the first iteration. A single pass is never enough. It also shows the sensitivity to initialisation: starting at 2 and 4 put both centroids inside the same natural group, and the algorithm had to work its way out.

Choosing k

k-means cannot choose \(k\) for you; it must be given. Two standard approaches:

MethodHowLimitation
Elbow methodPlot SSE against k; look for the point where the decrease flattensThe elbow is often ambiguous or absent
Silhouette scoreFor each point, compare its mean distance to its own cluster against the nearest other cluster; average over all pointsCostlier to compute; still a heuristic
Domain knowledgeYou know there are 3 customer tiersRequires the knowledge

Silhouette for one point is \(s = \frac{b-a}{\max(a,b)}\) where \(a\) is the mean distance to its own cluster and \(b\) the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. It ranges from −1 (wrong cluster) through 0 (on the boundary) to +1 (well inside).

The five challenges of k-means

  1. k must be chosen in advance. The algorithm has no notion of how many clusters exist.
  2. Sensitive to initialisation. Different starting centroids can converge to different local optima with different SSE. k-means++ and multiple restarts mitigate this, not eliminate it.
  3. Assumes roughly spherical, equal-sized clusters. Because it minimises squared distance to a centre, it cannot represent elongated or ring-shaped clusters — it will cut a ring in half.
  4. Sensitive to outliers. A centroid is a mean, and a mean is dragged by extreme values. One far-away point can pull an entire cluster.
  5. Every point must be assigned. There is no notion of noise — a point that belongs to no cluster is forced into the nearest one anyway.
Exam trap

k-means always terminates but not always at the global optimum, and it always assigns every point — there are no outliers. Both are frequent true/false questions. If a dataset has genuinely unassignable points, DBSCAN (chapter 12) is the answer, and that is exactly the comparison to make.

Implementation

Pythonkmeans.py
from math import dist


def kmeans(points, k, centroids, max_iter=100):
    """Lloyd's algorithm. points and centroids are tuples."""
    for iteration in range(max_iter):
        # 1. assignment step
        clusters = [[] for _ in range(k)]
        for p in points:
            best = min(range(k), key=lambda i: dist(p, centroids[i]))
            clusters[best].append(p)

        # 2. update step
        new_centroids = []
        for i, group in enumerate(clusters):
            if not group:
                new_centroids.append(centroids[i])       # keep an empty cluster's centre
                continue
            dims = len(group[0])
            new_centroids.append(tuple(sum(p[d] for p in group) / len(group) for d in range(dims)))

        if new_centroids == centroids:                   # converged
            return clusters, centroids, iteration
        centroids = new_centroids

    return clusters, centroids, max_iter


def sse(clusters, centroids):
    return sum(dist(p, centroids[i]) ** 2 for i, g in enumerate(clusters) for p in g)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    pts = [(1, 1), (2, 1), (4, 3), (5, 4)]
    groups, cents, it = kmeans(pts, 2, [(1, 1), (5, 4)])
    print("iterations:", it)          # 1 (converged after the second assignment)
    print("centroids :", cents)       # [(1.5, 1.0), (4.5, 3.5)]
    print("SSE       :", sse(groups, cents))   # 1.5
Pythonwith scikit-learn
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

X = [[1, 1], [2, 1], [4, 3], [5, 4]]
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)

print(km.cluster_centers_)   # [[4.5 3.5] [1.5 1. ]]
print(km.labels_)            # cluster index per point
print(km.inertia_)           # 1.5 — this is SSE

Check your understanding

In the second solved example, why did point 4 move from cluster 2 to cluster 1 in iteration 2?
After iteration 1 the centroids are no longer the initial guesses — they are the means of their groups, 2.5 and 16. Distance from 4 to 2.5 is 1.5, to 16 is 12, so 4 now belongs to cluster 1. This is why a single pass is never sufficient: assignments depend on centroids, and centroids depend on assignments.
Which dataset would k-means handle badly?
k-means minimises squared distance to a centroid, so it can only produce convex, roughly spherical regions. Two concentric rings would be cut into left and right halves rather than inner and outer — the correct grouping is not linearly separable by distance to a centre. DBSCAN handles this correctly because it reasons about local density rather than global distance.
